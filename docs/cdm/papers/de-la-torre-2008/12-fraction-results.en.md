# Fractional subtraction result and counterexample

## Initial fitting

Under original Q:

\[
\overline{\widehat g}+\overline{\widehat s}=.2461.
\]

There are 30 \(g,s\) parameters in 15 questions, and all but 5 of them do not exceed .20. The authors regard this as a reasonable initial model-data representation under DINA.

## Threshold scan

For the 51 thresholds from \(.000\) to \(.050\), the author also draws:

1. \(\bar g+\bar s\) after update;
2. Consistent ratio of the whole row between old and new Q.

The result is:

\[
\varepsilon\in\{.009,.010,.011,.012\}
\]

When , the candidate Q is exactly the same as the original Q, and the corresponding \(\bar g+\bar s\) reaches the lowest interval.

The authors therefore retain the original property definitions and Q specifications.

## Figure 1 How to read

In the original Figure 1:

- The horizontal axis is \(\varepsilon\);
- The vertical axis is \(\bar g+\bar s\);
- The dashed line is the original value .2461;
- Very small threshold yields about .247--.248;
- .009--.012 is reduced to a minimum;
- As the threshold continues to increase, the indicator rises significantly to about .265.

An excessively large threshold will stop adding attributes prematurely, delete the attributes that are really needed, and cause the item parameters to deteriorate.

## Figure 2 How to read

Figure 2 compares the entire row consistency rate of the original Q and the candidate Q. Only in the range .009--.012:

\[
\text{row agreement}=1.
\]

Combining it with Figure 1, it shows that "retaining the original Q" simultaneously satisfies:

- The lowest statistical index of all tests;
- The original cognitive norms of each question are retained.

## Counterexample deliberately constructed by the author

The author then artificially added attribute 5 to question 1. Question 1 originally only required basic fraction subtraction:

\[
\boldsymbol q_1=10000.
\]

Attribute 5 "Convert integers to fractions" has nothing to do with this question from the item content. After adding it:

\[
\overline{\widehat g}+\overline{\widehat s}=.2379,
\]

Lower than the optimal value of the original Q

\[
.2461.
\]

If you only minimize by statistical indicators, the content of the wrong specifications will be selected.

## What does this counterexample illustrate?

The response data may have an attribute act as:

- Proxies for student group differences;
- Stands with unmodelled abilities;
- Stands for other attribute correlations;
- Accidental groupers in the current sample.

A lower \(g+s\) only means that the two components under this sample are further apart, and cannot automatically prove that "this question cognitively requires attribute 5".

## Application Conclusion

The fraction subtraction application provides two complementary results:

1. The reasonable threshold interval can completely retain the existing Q;
2. Pursuing lower statistical losses alone may lead to incorrect content interpretation.

This is also direct evidence that the author insists on using \(\delta\)-method in conjunction with expert knowledge, student oral reports, and other model fit checks.
