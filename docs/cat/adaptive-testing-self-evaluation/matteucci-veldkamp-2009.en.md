# Matteucci and Veldkamp (2009): How empirical prior changes both CAT estimation and topic selection paths

!!! abstract "Key takeaway"
    **What was done:** Matteucci and Veldkamp used simulated background variables to construct an individualized normal prior, and used Gibbs sampler to allow it to enter the initial position, question-by-question ability estimate and final score at the same time; the next question is still selected according to the maximum information amount at the current estimate. **What is gained:** The correct and predictive experience prior is most beneficial to the 5-question short test and extreme abilities. The advantage weakens as the number of questions increases; it is better to let the prior continue to update just changing the starting point. **Conclusion for this topic:** "Standard maximum information topic selection will also be personalized when estimates are changed" has been done; new contributions must come from text or self-assessment information, error prior risk, variable length stop, or new topic selection criteria using full posterior.

## Citation details

> Matteucci, M., & Veldkamp, B. P. (2009). *Computer Adaptive Testing with Empirical Prior Information: A Gibbs Sampler Approach for Ability Estimation*. [Full text of the paper](https://amsacta.unibo.it/id/eprint/2659/1/matteucci_veldkamp_CAT_2009.pdf)

- Research type: Method paper and Monte Carlo simulation study
- Reaction model: single-dimensional two-parameter normal-ogive model (two-parameter normal-ogive model, 2PNO)
- Core method: use background variables to construct individualized normal priors, and then use Gibbs sampler to complete the question-by-question and final ability estimate
- Topic selection rules: Always use the maximum information rule at the current ability estimate
- Important limitation: background variables are covariates generated by simulation, not examinee self-evaluation; all tests use fixed length

## 1. How much further does it go than van der Linden (1999)?

[van der Linden (1999)](van-der-linden-1999.md) solves how to estimate from "auxiliary variables + question-by-question responses":

\[
\theta_i\mid X_i
\sim
N(X_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2).
\]

His real-data example shows that this individualized conditional distribution can be estimated, but does not use it to rerun CAT and compare the results.

Matteucci and Veldkamp (2009) actually put this distribution into simulated CAT:

1. Initialize the ability position with the conditional mean;
2. Select the first question at this position;
3. After each answer, use the same individualized prior and the current answer likelihood to update the ability;
4. Then choose the next question with the maximum amount of information in the updated ability position;
5. The final ability estimate also retains this prior.

Therefore, it is no longer a "only change the first question" design. Even if the maximum information content formula has not changed, the current ability estimate entering the formula has changed, and the actual item sequence will also change accordingly.

|question| van der Linden（1999） |Matteucci and Veldkamp (2009)|
|---|---|---|
|where does individualized prior come from|Joint estimation from true question-by-question responses and auxiliary variables|It is assumed in the simulation that the regression parameters and prior variance are known|
|Whether to actually run individualized CAT|No|Yes|
|prior Whether to enter question-by-question estimation|Suggest two possible uses|Yes, updated with Gibbs sampler|
|Whether to compare CAT performance|No|Yes, relatively fixed questions 5, 10, 15, 20|
|Whether to check for errors prior|No|No|

## 2. CAT’s reaction model and four steps

The paper uses 2PNO. For item \(j\):

\[
P(Y_j=1\mid\theta)
=
\Phi(\alpha_j\theta-\delta_j),
\]

Among them:

- \(Y_j=1\) means correct answer;
- \(\alpha_j\) is the discrimination;
- \(\delta_j\) is difficulty parameter;
- \(\Phi(\cdot)\) is the standard normal cumulative distribution function.

CAT follows a four-step cycle:

1. Initialization capability;
2. Select item;
3. Administer the test and record the response;
4. Update ability estimate.

Repeat steps 2 to 4 until the stopping rule is reached. This article does not use precision stops, but instead fixes them at 5, 10, 15, or 20 questions respectively.

## 3. How do background variables become individualized prior?

The author assumes that linear regression is satisfied between ability and \(P\) background variables:

\[
\theta_i
=
\beta_0+\beta_1X_{i1}+\cdots+\beta_PX_{iP}+\varepsilon_i,
\qquad
\varepsilon_i\sim N(0,\sigma^2).
\]

So:

\[
\theta_i\mid X_i
\sim
N(\mu_i,\sigma^2),
\qquad
\mu_i=X_i^\top\beta.
\]

This conditional distribution plays two roles.

### Role 1: Initialization

For new examinees, the initial capabilities are set to:

\[
\widehat\theta_i^{(0)}=\mu_i=X_i^\top\widehat\beta.
\]

The first question is chosen in this individualized position, instead of everyone starting from \(0\).

### Role 2: Question by question and final estimate

Complete:

\[
N(\mu_i,\sigma^2)
\]

is retained as Bayesian prior. Here \(\mu_i\) determines the prior center, and \(\sigma^2\) determines how much the system should trust the background information.

!!! important "Why does prior automatically change the topic selection?"
    Question \(k\) is still selected based on the maximum amount of information at the current ability estimate:

    \[
    j_k
    =
    \arg\max_{j\in R_k}
    I_j(\widehat\theta_{k-1}).
    \]

    individualized prior changes \(\widehat\theta_{k-1}\), so even if this rule \(\arg\max I_j(\cdot)\) remains unchanged, the ability position entering the rule and the final selected item will also change. "The topic selection formula has not been modified" does not mean "the topic selection path has not been modified."

## 4. How to update the ability of Gibbs sampler

### Step 1: Write the binary response as a latent continuous variable

2PNO can be represented by data augmentation. Introduce latent variable for each administered test question:

\[
Z_j\mid\theta
\sim
N(\alpha_j\theta-\delta_j,1),
\]

And order:

\[
Y_j=
\begin{cases}
1,&Z_j>0,\\
0,&Z_j\leq 0.
\end{cases}
\]

Given the answer result, the conditional distribution of \(Z_j\) is truncated normal: when the answer is correct, it is cut off above \(0\), and when the answer is wrong, it is cut off below \(0\).

### Step 2: Update \(\theta\) when given \(Z\)

By:

\[
Z_j+\delta_j
=
\alpha_j\theta+v_j,
\qquad
v_j\sim N(0,1),
\]

You can get the normal likelihood contributed only by the current answer:

\[
\theta\mid Z,\xi
\sim
N(\widehat\theta_L,v),
\]

Among them:

\[
v
=
\left(\sum_{j\in S_k}\alpha_j^2\right)^{-1},
\qquad
\widehat\theta_L
=
\frac{
\sum_{j\in S_k}\alpha_j(Z_j+\delta_j)
}{
\sum_{j\in S_k}\alpha_j^2
}.
\]

Then with individualized prior:

\[
\theta\mid X_i\sim N(\mu_i,\sigma^2)
\]

Combined, we get:

\[
\theta\mid Z,\xi,X_i
\sim
N(m_k,V_k),
\]

\[
V_k
=
\left(
\frac{1}{v}+
\frac{1}{\sigma^2}
\right)^{-1},
\qquad
m_k
=
V_k\left(
\frac{\widehat\theta_L}{v}
+
\frac{\mu_i}{\sigma^2}
\right).
\]

This formula is very intuitive: the posterior mean is the accuracy weighted average of "response evidence" and "background information prediction".

- When there are few items, \(v\) is larger and the priority weight is higher;
- As information gradually accumulates, \(v\) becomes smaller and the weight of response evidence increases;
- The smaller the \(\sigma^2\) is, the more the system believes in the background information;
- If \(\mu_i\) is far wrong and \(\sigma^2\) is small, the error prior will be more difficult to correct by the item.

### Step 3: Alternate Sampling

Each time the capabilities are updated, the algorithm is executed alternately:

1. Given the current \(\theta\) and answer, extract all \(Z_j\) from the truncated normal;
2. Given \(Z\), item parameters and individualized prior, extract new \(\theta\) from \(N(m_k,V_k)\);
3. Repeat until you have a posterior sample of ability for the current question.

The item parameters \(\xi\), regression coefficient \(\beta\) and residual variance \(\sigma^2\) are all considered known in the CAT stage, so the Gibbs sampler here only updates \(Z\) and \(\theta\).

!!! note "Don't use the Gibbs sampler as a source of effects"
    Gibbs sampler is a tool for calculating posterior. The main reason why the empirical conditions in the paper are better than the standard conditions is that the former uses a correctly specified prior that is related to the real ability; it is not because "MCMC is inherently more accurate than other ability estimate methods." If the same model is calculated using analytical posteriors, numerical integration, or other reliable Bayesian methods, the same target posterior should be obtained.

## 5. How to set up simulation

|design elements|Paper setting|
|---|---|
|item bank|200 two-point questions|
|Discrimination| \(\alpha_j\sim U(0.7,2)\) |
|difficulty parameter| \(\delta_j\sim U(-3,3)\) |
|Ability points| \(-3,-2.5,-2,-1,0,1,2,2.5,3\) |
|Background variable relationship| \(\theta=0.2+0.7X+\varepsilon\)，\(\varepsilon\sim N(0,0.3)\) |
|test length|Questions 5, 10, 15, 20|
|Topic selection|Both groups use the maximum amount of information at the current ability position|
| Gibbs chain |5,000 iterations, first 500 as burn-in|
|Repeat times|100 times per condition|
|result indicator|posterior mean, posterior standard deviation, bias, root mean squared error (root mean square error, RMSE)|

This is an idealized simulation that favors the empirical prior: the linear relationship used to generate the data is consistent with the prior model used in the analysis, and the prediction coefficients and prior variance are treated as known. The paper does not simulate self-evaluation errors, regression mismatches, prior mean systematic shifts or prior variance estimation errors.

## 6. What has been changed in the three comparison conditions?

|Conditions|Initial ability|Question by question and final prior|Topic selection with maximum amount of information|
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard | \(0\) | \(N(0,1)\) |Yes|
| Semi-empirical | \(\mu_i=X_i^\top\beta\) |Standard prior, do not continue to use background information|Yes|
| Empirical | \(\mu_i=X_i^\top\beta\) | \(N(\mu_i,\sigma^2)\) |Yes|

The first simulation only compared Standard and Empirical and varied the fixed number of questions. The second item is fixed at 5 questions, and Semi-empirical is added to distinguish between "only changing the starting point" and "letting the prior continue to participate in the estimation".

This is already a useful ablation, but is not a complete \(2\times2\) factorial design. In particular, the "standard first question + individualized prior" condition is not set separately; nor is the prior mean accuracy and prior variance manipulated separately.

## 7. Main results under different question sizes

### Question 5: The advantage of experience prior is the most obvious

The following are excerpts from three representative capability points in Table 1:

|True \(\theta\)|Empirical: estimate / bias / RMSE|Standard: estimate/bias/RMSE|
|---:|---:|---:|
| \(-3\) | \(-3.09/-0.09/0.42\) | \(-2.33/0.67/0.69\) |
| \(0\) | \(-0.08/-0.08/0.31\) | \(0.04/0.04/0.33\) |
| \(3\) | \(3.13/0.13/0.44\) | \(2.23/-0.77/0.78\) |

When there are only 5 questions, the standard normal prior obviously pulls the extreme ability back to \(0\). The correct individualized prior can move the center toward the individual's true position, so the bias and RMSE of extreme abilities are significantly reduced. When the ability is at the center, there is little difference between the two methods.

### Question 10: Empirical conditions still usually have lower RMSE

After the response information is increased, the extreme bias of Standard is significantly reduced. Empirical still has lower RMSE in most ability points, but the advantage is smaller than that of 5 questions.

### Questions 15 and 20: The prior advantage is weakened, and bias is no longer dominant in one direction.

As the number of items increases, the standard conditions gradually catch up. The author also admits that Standard's posterior mean and bias are slightly better than Empirical's at some locations.

More importantly, the discussion section of the paper claims that Empirical's standard deviation and RMSE are consistently better, but Table 4 does not fully support this generalization. For example, for 20 questions:

|True \(\theta\)| Empirical RMSE | Standard RMSE |Whichever is lower|
|---:|---:|---:|---|
| \(-3\) | 0.38 | 0.27 | Standard |
| \(0\) | 0.15 | 0.16 |Empirical, the difference is small|
| \(3\) | 0.33 | 0.28 | Standard |

Therefore, the safest summary is: **The correct empirical prior has obvious advantages in very short tests; the advantage shrinks as the number of questions increases, and not every ability point and every indicator remains dominant. **

## 8. The ablation results of “only changing the starting point” and “complete prior”

Table 5 is fixed to 5 questions, comparing three conditions. Take RMSE as an example:

|True \(\theta\)| Empirical | Semi-empirical | Standard |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| \(-3\) | 0.42 | 0.71 | 0.69 |
| \(0\) | 0.31 | 0.29 | 0.33 |
| \(3\) | 0.44 | 0.72 | 0.78 |

In general, the full Empirical condition is most stable away from \(0\); but the Semi-empirical condition is not mechanically in between the other two:

- At \(\theta=-3\), the RMSE of Semi-empirical is even slightly higher than Standard;
- Semi-empirical has the lowest RMSE at \(\theta=0\);
- At \(\theta=3\) it is clearly between Empirical and Standard.

This does not overturn the overall advantage of complete prior on very short tests, but it shows that "just changing the starting point will definitely produce a centering effect" is not a rule that is established point by point. With only 100 replicates per condition, some small differences may also contain Monte Carlo fluctuations.

## 9. What does the MCMC convergence check do?

The author compares 1,000, 2,000, 5,000 and 10,000 iterations under the condition of \(\theta=0\), 5 questions, and uses:

- trace plot；
- autocorrelation plot；
- Monte Carlo standard error；
- The empirical criterion "Monte Carlo error is less than 5% of the posterior standard deviation".

1,000 iterations did not meet this criterion; 2,000 iterations barely met it; 5,000 and 10,000 iterations both met it. Therefore, the formal simulation uses 5,000 iterations and 500 burn-in.

This section can only illustrate that the Monte Carlo error for 5,000 samples is small for the simple one-dimensional conditions examined. It uses a single chain, a single ability point, and a single question size, and does not report multi-chain \(\widehat R\), effective sample size, or diagnosis of all extreme conditions, so it cannot be cited as "the algorithm has proven convergence in any CAT."

## 10. What does the paper really prove?

The paper directly supports:

1. Individualized experience prior can be used for CAT initialization, question-by-question estimation and final estimation at the same time;
2. After changing the current ability estimate a priori, even if the classic maximum information rule is still used for topic selection, the actual topic selection path will also change;
3. Under correctly specified priors with strong predictive power, 5-question short tests and extreme ability points can achieve significant bias or RMSE improvements;
4. Using auxiliary information only at the beginning is generally less effective than letting the auxiliary information continue to enter the posterior estimate.

The paper does not directly support:

- examinee self-assessment can accurately generate this prior;
- Individualized prior will reduce the number of questions under the same precision stopping rule;
- The algorithm is still safe when the prior mean is wrong;
- A certain fixed prior variance is suitable for different groups of people;
- Experience initialization will definitely reduce the first question or overall exposure;
- Gibbs sampler itself is more accurate than EAP, MAP or numerical integration;
- The result can be directly generalized to multidimensional, hierarchical scoring, content constraints or clinical CAT.

!!! warning "fixed test length cannot answer whether to stop earlier"
    The four test lengths are fixed length conditions that are run separately, not the same field. CAT stops itself based on the posterior standard error. Therefore, this article can only compare "who is more accurate after completing the same number of questions", but cannot compare "how many questions are needed to achieve the same accuracy."

## 11. Honest judgment of the novelty of our research

This paper disqualifies the following claim as novel:

> For the first time, individualized priors affect both the CAT ability estimate and the actual topic selection path.

Because this article has been approved:

\[
X_i
\longrightarrow
N(\mu_i,\sigma^2)
\longrightarrow
\widehat\theta_{i,k}
\longrightarrow
\arg\max_j I_j(\widehat\theta_{i,k})
\]

Completed the chain.

But it still leaves clear space for our research:

|Matteucci and Veldkamp No study|We can check directly|
|---|---|
|Background variable is not self-assessment or active difficulty selection|How much real signal does self-assessment grade have?|
|prior is completely correctly specified|Mean shifts, binning errors, and systematic over/underestimation|
|Prior variance is basically fixed|Sensitivity analysis such as \(\tau^2\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,1\}\)|
|fixed test length|Compare question volumes under the same posterior variance or standard error threshold|
|No item exposure result|First question exposure, overall exposure, conditional exposure and test overlap|
|Maximum amount of information from the first question, possible immediate use of high \(a\) questions|Interaction of prior reliability with \(a\)-stratified or posterior-variance gating|
|No separate control of \(b\)-matching candidate set|Randomize within the item set matching \(b\) or select by \(a\) layer|

The most valuable question is no longer “Can a prior get into CAT?”, but:

> When the prior comes from self-assessment that may be wrong, how to control the prior strength, early item discrimination and randomization of candidate questions at the same time, so that CAT can shorten the number of questions without increasing bias, early stopping of errors and local exposure?

## 12. Direct inspiration for subsequent ablation design

At a minimum, the following three groups should be retained to reproduce the core comparisons of this article:

1. Standard: \(N(0,1)\) initialization and update;
2. Start-only: The self-evaluation only changes the initial position, and subsequently restores the standard estimate;
3. Full prior: Self-evaluation determines \(\mu_i\) and \(\tau_i^2\) at the same time, and continues to update.

Then gradually add our own mechanisms:

4. Full prior + randomization within the starting candidate set, used to disperse the exposure of the first question;
5. Full prior + low \(a\) starting or \(a\) layer gating based on posterior variance;
6. Full prior + candidate set randomization + \(a\) layer gating.

All methods should use the same item bank, the same content constraints, and the same stopping rule, and report at the same time:

- bias, RMSE and interval coverage rate;
- average and tail test length;
- Error early stopping rate;
- Overall and conditional exposure rates by real ability and self-rated groups;
- test overlap；
- The interaction between self-assessment accuracy and prior variance.

## 13. Chinese paraphrasing that can be safely included in the paper

### Empirical prior and actual topic selection path

> Even if individualizedempirical prior does not change the maximum information item selection formula, it will change the actual item selection path by changing the item-by-item ability estimate (Matteucci & Veldkamp, 2009).

### Earnings in very short quizzes

> In simulations where the prior model is correctly specified, auxiliary variables are used for both initialization and ability estimates, which mainly improves the estimation accuracy of extremely short tests and extreme ability positions; as the number of questions increases, the advantage of the empirical prior over the standard prior weakens (Matteucci & Veldkamp, 2009).

### Only change the starting point and complete prior

> A simulation of a 5-question CAT shows that initializing with only auxiliary information is generally inferior to letting individualized priors continue into the question-by-question and final ability estimates; however, the item-by-item results of each ability point do not always show a strict intermediate relationship (Matteucci & Veldkamp, 2009).

### Evidence Boundary

> This study used fixed test length and correctly specified simulation priors, so it did not directly prove that real self-assessment can shorten the variable length CAT, nor did it evaluate the risk of incorrect priors and item exposure (Matteucci & Veldkamp, 2009).

## 14. Conclusion after intensive reading

This paper is very close to this topic, but it answers a question under ideal conditions: If we already have a correct and predictive individualized prior, is it beneficial to keep it in CAT? **The answer is that it tends to be advantageous at very short tests and at extreme abilities.

What we really haven't solved yet is a more difficult and realistic problem: **When the prior comes from the examinee itself and may be biased greatly, how to control its intensity and how to arrange the first questions to achieve a verifiable balance between efficiency, accuracy and exposure. **
