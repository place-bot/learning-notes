# Rejection Sampling iterative fine-tuning

## 1. Basic algorithm

For each prompt \(p\), sample \(K\) answers from the current policy \(\pi\):

\[
y^{(1)},\ldots,y^{(K)}
\sim
\pi(\cdot\mid p).
\]

reward model scoring:

\[
s_k=r(p,y^{(k)}).
\]

Choose the answer with the highest score:

\[
y^*
=
\arg\max_{1\le k\le K}s_k.
\]

Treat \((p,y^*)\) as new gold data, and then perform gradient update similar to SFT.

## 2. Why it works

If the probability of producing a good answer for each sampling is \(q\), the probability of at least one good answer is:

\[
1-(1-q)^K.
\]

When \(K\) is larger, the chance of exploring high-quality trajectories increases. Figure 7 of the paper shows that the maximum reward increases with the number of samples, while the median is basically stable; the difference between the two curves is the potential benefit of reranking.

## 3. Temperature and exploration

The sampling distribution is:

\[
p_\tau(y_t\mid c)
=
\operatorname{softmax}
\left(\frac{z_t}{\tau}\right).
\]

Higher temperatures expand diversity, allowing \(K\) answers to cover more candidates; too high temperatures will reduce the quality of individual answers. The paper found that the optimal temperature will change with the RLHF version; later models often obtain higher maximum rewards with \(1.2\)–\(1.3\) when \(K=10\) to 100.

## 4. Only use 70B to perform rejection sampling

The paper only allows a maximum of 70B to generate and filter rejection-sampling data. The smaller 7B/13B model is fine-tuned on the 70B selected data, which is equivalent to distilling the capabilities of the larger model into the small model.

## 5. Breadth and depth

### breadth of Rejection sampling

The same policy explores \(K\) answers for each prompt at once, and then trains them uniformly.

### Depth of PPO

The policy changes after each gradient update, and the next round of samples comes from the updated model, forming a continuous trajectory optimization.

The paper performs multiple rounds of rejection-sampling updates, making the practical difference between the two smaller than a one-time algorithm comparison.

## 6. Forgetting problem

The early RLHF-V3 only used new data generated by the previous version, and there was a regression in the ability to write rhymes and poems. Subsequent iterations add high-scoring samples from all historical versions together:

\[
\mathcal D_t
=
\bigcup_{j=1}^{t}
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{best}}^{(j)}.
\]

History replay alleviates training distribution narrowing and ability forgetting.

## 7. Algorithm pseudocode

```text
Input: current policy π_t, reward model r_t, prompt set P
best_set = historical high score samples

for p in P:
    samples = sample K answers from π_t(·|p)
    y_star = reward highest answer
    best_set join (p, y_star)

π_{t+1} = perform answer-only SFT on best_set
```

## 8. Risks

- RM bias will be amplified;
- The more samples, the easier it is to find the text of the exploit RM;
- top-1 discards information from other candidates;
- High computational costs focus on generation and scoring;
- Only distilling from 70B may pass on its bias;
- If new data replaces old data too much, it will be forgotten.

The paper uses continuous update RM, historical replay, manual review and follow-up PPO to alleviate these problems.
